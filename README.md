# AI & YOU

## An AI Awareness and Judgment Lab for Grades 6–12

**Created by Cheryl Abram | Ypifany**

AI is already part of how young people find information, make things, and interact with the world. AI & YOU gives them opportunities to explore that involvement through real tasks—not just definitions or instructions about what to believe.

Students use approved AI tools, compare responses, create things, and examine what they choose to accept, change, share, or leave unresolved.

**[Experience the AI & YOU website](https://ypifany.github.io/ai-and-you/)**

## The idea behind the course

> AI supplies a response. I determine whether and how that response serves as an answer to my question. My interpretation, acceptance, and use remain my responsibility.

Students practice that distinction by working with images, messages, information, plans, and creative projects.

They also consider what information a tool needs, who else is affected, and what permission is required before a tool takes action.

The goal is not to make students distrust every response or adopt a preferred opinion about AI. It is to give them opportunities to investigate, make choices, and explain their thinking.

## Try the public preview

The website opens with two short experiences:

### AI wrote this. Would you use it?

Read AI-generated lines for a fictional school-club invitation. Choose whether to use them, edit them, use something else, or remain unsure.

There is no score and no required answer.

### A draft is ready. May AI send it?

Explore the difference between creating a draft and authorizing an action. Review the message, audience, and permission before deciding what happens next.

This is a clearly labeled simulation. No messages are sent and no accounts are connected.

Both activities use prepared examples, not live AI generation. The full course includes interaction with approved AI tools throughout.

## What the course covers

| Session | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | AI in everyday life |
| 2 | Checking information before relying on it |
| 3 | Comparing responses and examining representation |
| 4 | Planning with AI and choosing appropriate access |
| 5 | Revising requests and noticing where instructions come from |
| 6 | Consent, credit, disclosure, and ethical sharing |
| 7 | Supervising AI actions, setting limits, and getting help |
| 8 | Creating, reviewing, and explaining a final project |

## Designed for different settings

The reference format is eight one-hour sessions, with options to combine sessions or extend projects.

The program is designed for schools, nonprofits, after-school programs, and community organizations. It supports face-to-face, virtual, and blended delivery.

Students can talk, write, or make something. Turning on a camera or speaking to the whole group is not required.

Examples become locally relevant through a student-created **Local Swap Card**, which brings familiar places, activities, rules, events, and checkable facts into the work.

No coding is required. Tools must be appropriate and approved for the setting.

## Accessibility and privacy

The website is built with WCAG 2.2 Level AA as its accessibility goal, not a certification. Further screen-reader and user testing is still needed.

The preview includes keyboard-operable controls, text alternatives for material previews, and readable content without completing the activities.

It does not require an account, collect activity responses, or submit visitors’ writing to an AI service. The website host may retain normal access logs, and linked websites have their own privacy practices.

Please do not enter names, passwords, private messages, or other sensitive information into the sample activities.
